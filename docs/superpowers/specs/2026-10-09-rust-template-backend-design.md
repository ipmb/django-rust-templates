## 1. The goal

A third-party Django package that a project turns on by naming it in
`TEMPLATES`:

```python
TEMPLATES = [{
    "BACKEND": "django_rust_templates.rust.RustTemplates",
    "DIRS": [BASE_DIR / "templates"],
    "APP_DIRS": True,
    "OPTIONS": {},
}]
```

`pip install` and that line, and nothing else — no entry point to edit, no
`sitecustomize.py`, no `install()`, no ordering rule. It must be faster than the
Python implementation it replaces.

*The `"NAME"` line was not in the original snippet, and its absence was a live bug
in the README and in every packaging fixture until M18 measured it — but the bug
was the **path**, not the omission. The module was `backends.py`, so the derived
alias was `backends` and an entry that named nothing was reachable only as
`engines["backends"]`. The repair at the time was to spell `"NAME"` everywhere;
the repair that holds is the rename to `rust.py` (§2.2 of `.review/m18-record.md`),
which is why the snippet above carries no `"NAME"` and needs none.*

## 2. Why this is a rewrite of the interface and not a rename

The package today is not a backend. It is a **substitution for `django.template`**:
a meta-path finder serves a transcription of Django's template package under
Django's own module names, and `install()` is the seam that turns it on. That
design was chosen to keep Django's own `tests/template_tests/` suite (1547 tests)
running unmodified as the acceptance criterion.

Three measurements (Django 6.0.9, CPython 3.14.4, `target/packaging/venv-match`)
say the two interfaces are not interchangeable, and that the substitution is what
costs the time.

> **M18 note — read §0's translation rule carefully here, because §2 is where it
> is most tempted to lie.** The three subsections below argue about a *mechanism*
> — a finder that serves our modules under Django's names — and their evidence is
> of a kind that does not survive being renamed. §2.2 is the clearest case: "a
> 16-case battery passed 16/16 while parsing with stock Django's `Parser`" is a
> claim that the old route could be green and wrong. The M18 successor has a
> counterpart property, and it is **not** "the vendored suite refuses to run

> ours by construction, and the tree is re-derived and diffed by
> `scripts/gen_django_suite.py --check` so a hand edit cannot hide. A reader who
> translates §2.2's sentence into the present tense and keeps its force will
> believe a guarantee this document never had.

### 2.1 A backend cannot be the activation point

Django imports a backend class lazily, when an engine alias is first looked up —
which is after `django.setup()`. Counting modules whose name is `django.template`
or begins with `django.template.` or `django.templatetags`, `django.setup()` leaves
**20** in `sys.modules` (17 under `django.template` — the package itself and its
sixteen submodules — plus three under `django.templatetags`), and importing
`django.template.backends.django` afterwards adds none of them: they were all
already there. `install()` refuses with `ShimError` naming the stale captures. The
mechanism is the same one that rules out a settings module
(`django.setup()` imports `django.urls`, which imports `django.template`, *before*
it reads settings) and `AppConfig.ready()` (later still). Measured; transcript in
`.review/m18-measurements/`.

### 2.2 Without the finder, a backend is served *stock Django*

`_impl` is a transcription that delegates **by name**. It resolves its default
builtins from the strings `django.template.defaulttags`, `…defaultfilters`,
`…loader_tags` and its loaders from `django.template.loaders.cached.Loader`; and
26 `from django.template …` imports are spread across twelve files:
`_impl/parser.py` (10), `_impl/engine.py` (3), `_impl/templatetags/i18n.py` (3),
`_impl/loaders/cached.py` (2), and eight more files with one apiece. With no finder
installed, every one of those resolves to
stock Django, silently. A 16-case battery written against exactly such a backend
passed 16/16 while parsing with **stock Django's** `Parser` and producing a tree
whose every node was `django.template.*`. The discriminator is `__file__`, not the
module name: under the finder and without it, every node prints as
`django.template.base`.

### 2.3 `_impl` has no importable identity

Importing `django_rust_templates._impl` by its real name fails at
`_impl/__init__.py` (`from . import autoreload`; there is no
`_impl/autoreload.py`, and the fall-through to Django's file only works because
the finder gives `_impl.__path__` a second entry). The transcription exists only
as the body served under the name `django.template`.

## 3. The measurement this milestone is built on

`.review/m18-measurements/m18_parse_split.py` splits a template's **compile** into
its stages. On our side the stages nest; stock has no seam, so it prints the one
column it has and the subtraction is the reader's. All four workloads render
byte-identical output on both sides (the sha256 column), so the comparison is
like for like. The `materialise` column is `our compile − Rust parser_for`, so it
understates materialisation by one `Parser` construction — immaterial against
2175 µs, and in the safe direction, since it understates the number that justifies
the work. The 91% below is a floor.

| workload | chars | nodes | Rust `lex` | Rust `parser_for` | our compile | **Python materialise** | stock compile | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| tiny | 13 | 1 | 0.38 | 1.66 | 22.84 | **21.18** | 11.41 | 2.00× |
| base | 208 | 8 | 2.79 | 6.02 | 48.85 | **42.83** | 20.81 | 2.35× |
| page | 382 | 1 | 5.13 | 11.08 | 115.23 | **104.15** | 78.65 | 1.47× |
| large | 9294 | 462 | 125.49 | **221.85** | 2397.07 | **2175.22** | 1492.81 | 1.61× |

**The Rust parser is already ~7× faster than the thing it replaces.** Lexing and
parsing a 9.3 KB, 462-node template costs 222 µs. Stock Django's *entire* compile
of the same template — lex, parse, and build every node — costs 1493 µs.

**And then 91% of our compile is spent throwing that away.** Turning the Rust
descriptor tree back into Python `Node` objects costs 2175 µs on `large`: more
than stock Django spends doing the whole job. On `tiny`, 21.2 µs of Python to hand
back a single variable node, against stock's 11.4 µs for everything.

This is the explanation of M16's four ratios, and the reason its three levers
could not reach the criterion: they all aimed at the render, while the parse cost
is dominated by a step that exists **only because `django.template` must hand back
a `.nodelist` of `Node` objects**.

### 3.1 What the materialisation is, and what "the tree" is

Two facts from the pinned tree, and both change the shape of §4.

**Materialisation is Python.** `_native.compile_template` does not convert the
tree in Rust. It calls `tree.materialise(desc, origin, resolve_ref, register_ref,
parser)`, and that resolves to `python/django_rust_templates/_impl/parser.py` — a
Python module, and the single file carrying 10 of §2.2's 26 imports. So the 2175 µs
column is a Python walk over the descriptor tree: 462 nodes at ~4.7 µs apiece, each
one a `cls(**kwargs)`. It is not a Rust cost, and no Rust change fixes it by
itself; it is the engine voluntarily re-entering Python 462 times — a cost the
current architecture **chose**, not one this measurement discovered. "The
materialiser is slow" and "we call into Python 462 times by design" invite
different fixes, and only the second is true.

**And the tree is not a renderable AST. It is a construction recipe.** `NodeDesc`
is `class_name: String`, `attrs: Vec<(String, AttrValue)>`, `nodelists: Vec<(String,
NodeListDesc)>`, plus `must_be_first`, `token` and `slot`. The `class_name` field is
documented as *"the Python class to materialise"*; `attrs` are keyed by
**constructor parameter name**; `nodelists` by **the attribute name the class's
`__init__` takes**. `{% for %}` emits `NodeDesc::new("ForNode").attr("loopvars", …)`.
The tree records what to *call*, not what to *do*.

The parser can emit class names, but there are **three** quantities here, not one, and
the first draft of this spec gave one of them with no method — which is how it came out
as the wrong number. Measured on the committed tree at `bb7e53d`, over `crates/`, by
`.review/m18-measurements/m18_census.py`, which re-derives all three at any revision:

| quantity | count | how it was counted |
| --- | --- | --- |
| emission call sites | **53** | `NodeDesc::new(` and `NodeDesc::with_nodelist(` — 41 production, 12 under `#[cfg(test)]` |
| distinct class-name literals | **44** | first argument of those calls — 39 production, 5 test-only |
| `NODE_CLASSES` entries | **40** | the dict literal in `_impl/parser.py`, read by `ast` |

The method is load-bearing, because **a line-oriented search for those literals finds
43, not 44**: `crates/template-core/src/parser/tags/mod.rs:219` opens
`NodeDesc::with_nodelist(` and puts the literal on the following line, where a per-line
grep cannot see it. A count whose instrument is unstated is a count of the instrument.

This census was counted wrongly four times by four methods, and each fix introduced the
next failure: a per-line search missed the multi-line call (43 names); scanning whole
text then swallowed a *comment* that writes the constructor name in prose — two phantom
sites from one line, because it names both forms. That comment is the one M18.0's own
spike adds, warning that *"a renderer (or a refactor) driven by grepping for string
literals would silently miss"* `TextNode` — so the warning against grepping is itself
written in the form that breaks a grep, and it will be in the tree from the moment the
spike lands. The comment-aware exclusion is therefore load-bearing, not hygiene: the
numbers above are pinned to `bb7e53d`, and a naive scan of the next commit reports 55.
`m18_census.py` asserts those totals and, when they move, rescans the baseline and
prints the sites that changed — so the pin is watched rather than merely dated, and the
comment functions as a canary: a scan reporting 55 sites post-spike is comment-blind,
one reporting 53 has shown the exclusion works.
And the check for the phantom sites used `git grep` with `\|`, which is not alternation
there and fails as a silent empty with the exit status of a true absence. What settled it was not a fifth method but **printing
the sites** — `path:line`, the name, and the source line — because a comment, a
`#[cfg(test)]` stand-in and a multi-line call are each obvious in a list and invisible
in a count. `m18_census.py --list` prints exactly that and marks any comment-prose site
instead of dropping it silently. When a count has been wrong repeatedly, the next thing
to produce is the enumeration.

The set arithmetic, measured against `NODE_CLASSES` rather than derived from it:

- **all 39** production literals are `NODE_CLASSES` keys — there is no production node
  the materialiser cannot build;
- the **5** test-only literals — `StandinBlockNode`, `StandinFirstNode`,
  `StandinHostNode`, `StandinRefNode`, `X` — are not; they are `#[cfg(test)]` stand-ins;
- exactly **one** `NODE_CLASSES` key has no literal producer anywhere: **`TextNode`**.

`TextNode` is the node a template is mostly made of, and it is produced by no class-name
literal at all — only by the `NodeDesc::text()` helper. Its partner `HOST_NODE` (from
`NodeDesc::host()`) is likewise never a literal, and is not a `NODE_CLASSES` key either:
`materialise_node` tests that name *before* the table, because it names a node the host
built — the third-party-tag seam §4 describes, which therefore already exists and is
tested. **So a renderer — or a refactor — enumerating node kinds by grepping literals
misses text nodes, and the two helper-emitted kinds are exactly where the count is
wrong.** That is the enumeration a route-(b) estimate actually needs: not "how many
producers are there" but "how many are findable by the method used to change them".

Those three numbers size three different jobs, which is why collapsing them to one
figure is how an estimate goes wrong:

- route (b)'s **per-variant** work is the **39** production literals plus the two
  helper-emitted kinds — one `NodeKind` arm apiece;
- route (b)'s **per-site** work is the **41** production call sites, or **53** if the
  `#[cfg(test)]` sites are rewritten too (they construct descriptors to assert on, so
  they must be, if the descriptor is deleted);
- and removing a producer without removing its `NODE_CLASSES` row leaves an orphan that
  **nothing asserts against** — the 5 test-only literals are the only names outside the
  table today, so the table itself is the invariant that has to be kept in step by hand.

So M18.0 is not "add a renderer to a tree that is already there". It has two
routes, and the spike must say which one its number is about:

- **(a) Render off the descriptors as they are** — dispatch on `class_name`, read
  `attrs` by name. No parser change; every tag module and every parser test is
  untouched; a first measurement is days away rather than weeks. The price is
  string dispatch and name-keyed attribute lookup on every node of every render —
  which is part of what the spike is trying to measure, so it has to be measured
  *separately* and stated.
- **(b) Make the tree semantic** — a `NodeKind` enum in `template-core`, emitted by
  each tag module in place of the descriptor, so the renderer matches a tag rather
  than a string. This is what §4 ultimately wants, and it deletes the descriptor
  and the materialiser outright. It touches all 39 production producers plus the two
  helper-emitting sites, and the 12 `#[cfg(test)]` sites that construct descriptors to
  assert on `class_name` today.

The spike runs (a) to answer "can a Rust render walk beat stock?". If yes, (b) is
the named follow-up. If no, (b) still has to be considered before the design is
called dead, because (a)'s overhead is not (b)'s. Saying which of those two a number
is about is the difference between a measurement and a guess.

## 4. Architecture

**The tree stays in Rust.** A compiled template is a Rust object: the descriptor
tree, the reference table, the origin, the engine's options. Python holds an
opaque handle to it.

**Rendering walks that tree in Rust.** A Rust node type for every builtin tag and
for variables, text and filters. The renderer reaches into Python only where the
thing being reached for *is* Python.

**The Python/Rust boundary, stated as a rule.** Cross when, and only when, the
operand is a Python object:

| crosses | why |
|---|---|
| the `Context` (`make_context`'s, unchanged) | it is the API every third-party tag and filter is written against; it is not ours to replace |
| a variable's final value | it comes out of the context |
| a tag or filter from a library we do not own | the compile function and the `Node` are Python; `{% load %}`-ed third-party and `django.contrib.*` libraries land here |
| the output of a Python node | ditto |

Everything else does not cross: text, control flow, inheritance, `{% include %}`,
the builtin filters, the parser's own work.

**Three consequences worth naming.**

*Materialisation disappears for our own nodes.* `{% for %}`, `{% if %}`,
`{{ var }}`, `{% block %}`, `{% extends %}`, `{% include %}`, filters — the
nodes a template is actually made of — are never Python objects. Third-party
library tags still are, because their compile functions run in Python and return
Python `Node`s; the tree holds a handle, and `NodeRef`/`NodeSlot` already exist for
exactly that.

*The per-application routing arm dies.* M16 measured `FilterExpression.resolve` at
1.404 µs per filter application, of which 1.061 is `django_own_filters()` merging
five module tuples on every call, to answer "is this filter Django's or ours?".
That question exists only because two universes can arrive at one call site. With
the filter's handle bound at compile time it is answered once and never asked
again.

*The join/re-split goes.* M16 attributes +0.595 µs per variable to
`_native.resolve` plus `".".join`. The join exists because `_native.resolve` takes
a dotted string; the Rust tree holds the lookups as a list, so the join and the
re-split are both removable.

### 4.1 Inheritance does not force materialisation, but it forces a carrier per block

This was the risk most likely to kill the design — if `{% extends %}` needs a
Python `Node` tree, then "the tree stays in Rust" is dead for every template that
inherits. It does not. Measured, `.review/m18-measurements/m18_block_carrier.py`,
5/5 on both sides, transcripts beside it.

Three mechanisms, all three checked from *outside* the engine, by a third-party
`takes_context` tag:

1. **The block census is compile-time and type-based.** `ExtendsNode.__init__`
   builds `self.blocks = {n.name: n for n in nodelist.get_nodes_by_type(BlockNode)}`.
   A Rust tree needs its own census of its own block nodes; nothing here needs
   Python.
2. **Finding the root is a walk.** `ExtendsNode.render` scans the parent's nodelist
   for the first non-text node and asks whether it is an `ExtendsNode`. Rust-side
   logic.
3. **One thing crosses, and it is the whole risk.** `BlockNode.render` puts the node
   itself into the context — measured: *inside* a block the tag sees
   `django.template.loader_tags.BlockNode`, name `content`, with `.super`; at top
   level it sees nothing. And `{{ block.super }}` reaches that object *through
   variable resolution*, which calls it if it is callable (`base.py`: `if
   callable(current): … current = current()`). Measured functionally, which is the
   part that does not depend on reading the source: the render produced
   `PARENT[CHILD(parent-body)]`, so the parent's body arrived and the bound method
   was called.

So a rendered `{% block %}` must place a small Python carrier in the context, and
that carrier's `super()` must be able to render the parent block. **The block body
stays in Rust**: the carrier is a handle and a callback, not a materialised
subtree. The cost is one object per rendered block, not one per node.

Two further measurements say the carrier is the *whole* of what crosses, and that
it is load-bearing from the first day:

- `BlockContext` and `BLOCK_CONTEXT_KEY` appear **nowhere in Django outside
  `loader_tags.py`** — not in `.django/django/`, in any module, at all. So the
  *container* is private to one file, and a Rust-resident block stack breaks
  nothing, because Django itself never reads that structure from outside. Only the
  per-block carrier has to be a real Python object.
- `block.super` occurs **38** times across **22** files in the acceptance suite
  (`template_tests/`). The carrier's callback path is exercised from day one, not a
  compatibility shim nobody hits.

### 4.2 Resolving a template at render time

`{% include %}` was the other construct that resolves a template during a render,
and one instrument answers it for the whole class of them.
`.review/m18-measurements/m18_include.py`, again using a `takes_context`-free
counting loader, 4/4 on both sides:

- **A template is recognised by duck typing, not by type.** `IncludeNode.render`
  tests `if not callable(getattr(template, "render", None))`. Measured: an object
  that is not a Django `Template` and merely has `render(context)` is accepted and
  called; it rendered `DUCK[hi]`.
- **The resolve is cached per *node*, and this is the design constraint.** One
  `{% include %}` inside a five-iteration loop loaded the template **once**; two
  separate `{% include %}` nodes naming the same template loaded it **twice**. The
  cache is `context.render_context.dicts[0]`, keyed by the node object, and
  `dicts[0]` is deliberately the outermost dict so it survives loop pushes. A Rust
  renderer can keep the same cache keyed by its own node index — but it has to
  reproduce the *scope*, which is one `Template.render`, not one process.
  (Without the second number the first means nothing: "loaded once in a loop" is
  equally consistent with a name-keyed cache.)
- **`{% include … only %}` isolates**, so a Rust renderer must be able to build the
  new context rather than only push onto the existing one.

**The two constructs disagree about how to recognise a template**, and that is the
finding: `IncludeNode` duck-types (`callable(getattr(template, "render", None))`)
while `ExtendsNode.get_parent` type-checks (`isinstance(parent, Template)` against
Django's own class). A backend has to satisfy both, and being duck-typed on one side
and `isinstance`-checked on the other means the wrapper obligation is not one rule
but a per-call-site one. Every construct that resolves a template during a render is
in this class; `{% include %}` and `{% extends %}` are two members and should be
probed as a class rather than one at a time.

Two ways to build it, and the second has a trap:

- (a) always build the carrier. Simple, always correct.
- (b) recognise `block` and `block.super` at parse time and compile them to native
  nodes, skipping the carrier — but only when nothing else can observe
  `context["block"]`, and a third-party `takes_context` tag or any Python-node
  subtree rendering against the same context can. Take this only with a test that
  pins the fallback.

Two further constraints this measurement places on the *backend*, not the tree:
`base.py`'s variable resolution reads `context.template.engine.string_if_invalid`,
so `context.template` and its `.engine` must exist and be ours; and
`ExtendsNode.get_parent` type-checks the resolved parent with `isinstance(parent,
Template)` against **Django's** `Template` class, so `{% extends some_var %}` where
the variable resolves to one of our compiled templates must be wrapped to satisfy
it, or the check owned on both sides.

One trap for whoever repeats this: the probe prints
`django.template.loader_tags.BlockNode` under the shim and under stock alike. Only
the `tree:` line discriminates.

> `django_rust_templates.engine` by import, not against `_impl` under a finder.

- `tests/milestone_ownership.py` and the M14 pre-flight that refuses a non-empty
  meter
- the version guard's *mechanism* (a finder installed too late) — the guard stays,
  because a transcription of one Django is still only proven against that one,
  but its trigger moves to backend construction
- `scripts/check_packaging.sh` cases 1, 1b and 5, which assert the activation
  ordering that no longer exists

**And one item is conditional, because §6 depends on it.** `host.py`'s
`django_own_tags()`, `django_own_filters()`, `effective_autoescape()` and
`effective_use_tz()` exist to answer "is this object Django's or ours?" at a call
site where either can arrive. If the shim survives as an opt-in (§6, item 2), that
ambiguity survives with it and **these stay**. They go only if the shim goes. The
first draft of this spec deleted them unconditionally while §6 kept the shim
running, which cannot both be true.

`python/django_rust_templates/_impl/` does not disappear, but it stops being
loadable *as* `django.template`: the 26 `from django.template …` imports become
relative, and the builtin and loader *name strings* are translated. It becomes a
normal package with an importable identity — which is what lets §6's item 2 run at
all.

## 6. The proof strategy — this is the part with real risk

**No corpus we write can replace the 1547, and the reason is authorship, not
coverage.** This project's round-1 finding 6 was exactly this shape: 47
hand-picked lexer cases tested the divergences their author thought of, and a
divergence nobody had imagined passed. The 1547's irreplaceable property is that it
was written by people who had never heard of this project, against a specification
they did not know would be reimplemented. It is the only oracle here with that
property. A differential corpus — however large, however randomised — is written by
the implementer, and the case it cannot contain is the case the implementer does not
know is a case.

So the goal is not to replace the 1547. It is to keep it running for as long as it
can still run, and to be honest about what the rest measures.

1. **A differential corpus.** Render a corpus of templates under this backend and
   under stock Django in two processes, and compare bytes. `scripts/differential.py`
   is this shape at 48 cases; it scales, and it is the only test that is about the
   *shipped configuration*. It is necessary and it is not sufficient — see above.
   Its value is that it can be randomised, which is the one thing a hand-written
   corpus cannot be.

   **The blind spot is not hypothetical, and it does not need a case we failed to
   imagine — it can be a case already in hand.** Our own `center` corpus contains one.
   `center/empty-value` is an empty value at width 5, so the margin is 5 and both the
   margin and the width are odd — the one parity where CPython's format spec and
   `str.center` genuinely disagree (`left = margin // 2` against
   `left = margin // 2 + (margin & width & 1)`). The value is empty, so there is
   nowhere for the extra space to go and both rules return five spaces. Two different
   rules, byte-identical output, on an input the corpus already holds. A differential
   corpus compares bytes: this row is green under a wrong implementation and green
   under the right one, and no amount of growth reaches it, because the input is
   already present and already agrees. That is the authorship argument above
   compressed into one measured row — and worse than the general form of it, since a
   corpus author cannot aim at a case whose observable is identical to the correct
   one, so finding one at all is luck rather than diligence.

   The row is instructive for a second reason. `center/both-odd-width`, the corpus's
   only row that does separate the two rules, exists because M4's plan read the rule
   and mandated it. `empty-value` exists for an unrelated reason and merely happens to
   sit on the blind spot. **The row that discriminates was designed; the row that
   exposes the oracle's limit arrived by accident.**

2. **The 1547, kept, running against `_impl`.** These tests import `django.template`
   and inspect our nodes, so they can only run with the finder installed — which
   makes them an opt-in developer mode. What they assert is that the *transcription*
   still matches the Django it was transcribed from. That is a real and continuing
   question, and they are the best available instrument for it. They are evidence
   about `_impl`, never evidence about the backend, and the README must say so where
   it reports them. **This is why §5 keeps the shim** rather than deleting it.

   > **M18 note — every clause of this item is now false, and the third sentence
   > is false in the *opposite* direction.** The shim was deleted. The 1547 are not
   > an opt-in developer mode: they are the acceptance run, invoked by
   > `scripts/check.sh` against the vendored copy in `django_suite/`. They do not
   > import `django.template`, and they are not evidence about `_impl` — `_impl`
   > does not exist. They are evidence about **this package's engine, under this
   > package's own name**, which is a stronger thing to be evidence about than the
   > transcription was, because it is the artefact a project installs. The
   > sentence to discard outright is the last one: §5 did not keep the shim, and
   > the question this item was deferring — whether `_impl` would drift from the
   > Rust tree with nothing comparing them — was answered by deleting one of the
   > two things that could drift.

   The question this raises, and which §5 defers rather than answers: does `_impl`
   surviving as a package create a second engine that drifts from the Rust tree
   with nothing comparing them? The honest answer is that the differential corpus
   renders through the *backend* only, so nothing would compare `_impl` to the Rust
   tree at all. Either something does, or the claim "the transcription is still
   Django's" is the only thing `_impl` is for and the Rust tree is trusted
   separately — and that should be a decision, not a residue.

3. **A reachability check on the Rust path.** The failure this design most invites is
   a template that quietly renders through a Python node because the Rust renderer
   declined it. The renderer must report which nodes it handled and which it
   delegated, with the **delegated set pinned**, so a node silently falling back
   shows as a set difference rather than as a passing render. Two conditions, both
   borrowed from this project's agreement gate, or the instrument lies:
   - it must have **seen something** — a reachability report that is empty because
     the instrument never ran agrees with "everything was handled";
   - its **unattributed list must be empty**, or "Rust handled all of them" can mean
     "the reporter could not classify any of them".

## 7. Milestones, with the stage gate

**M18.0 — the render spike, before anything else.** Build the Rust renderer and
measure `realistic/render` and `large` against stock, in the §2 configuration. §3.1
route (a) — render off the descriptors as they are — because it needs no parser
change and gets a number in days. Cover the 39 production `class_name` values the
corpus actually exercises and say which, and handle `TextNode` and `HostNode` explicitly:
§3.1 measures that neither is produced by a class-name literal, so a renderer or a
refactor driven by grepping literals will miss text nodes silently.

**The gate is on the dispatch-free floor, not on the renderer.** The first draft of
this section gated on "is the Rust renderer faster than stock, with the caveat that a
route-(a) result is about route (a)". That is not a stop condition. Its rebuttal —
"route (a)'s dispatch overhead is not route (b)'s" — is always in hand, because route
(b) does not exist to be measured, and a stop condition whose escape hatch is an
argument rather than a measurement cannot stop anything.

What is *invariant* between the two routes is the render work itself: resolving a
variable, calling a filter, appending to the output buffer, recursing. Whatever the
dispatch mechanism, every renderer does that work. So M18.0 measures three arms and
reports them **in this order**:

| arm | what it is |
|---|---|
| `floor` | the Rust walk with dispatch **removed** — the plan resolved at parse time, so no per-node `class_name` comparison and no name-keyed `attrs` lookup |
| `rust` | the same walk dispatching on `class_name`, as route (a) actually would |
| `stock`, `materialise` | Django's whole render; and our current Python-walk path, for scale |

**If `floor` is not faster than stock, neither route can win and the design is
dead.** `rust − floor` is the dispatch overhead, measured rather than asserted.
Reporting the floor *first* is the point of the ordering: if the dispatched number
comes first, the floor reads as its excuse instead of its gate.

Two things M18.0 still cannot establish, and should say in the same breath: it
measures the renderer in isolation, while the product claim is about a pipeline that
does not exist until the restructure lands; and a route-(a) number is about route (a)
even when the floor has already bounded both. **The gate authorises the work; it does
not establish the product.**

### 7.1 What M18.0 measured, and the scope of its verdict

Measured 2026-10-09. **The gate passes.** The floor beats stock's render on all four
workloads in every run: 0.9357 and 0.9379 at `mixed`/`large` in the run of record,
0.9357–0.9481 and 0.9354–0.9488 across four, `wins` 39–40 of 40. The dispatch tax is
under 1.2% of the render, so route (a)'s `class_name` comparison is free. The sharpest
number is `floor/walk` = 0.9723 / 0.9672: deleting a Python `for` loop over `Node`s and
replacing it with a Rust loop nets about 3%, because the floor pays in FFI crossings
what the deleted loop paid in bytecode.

**The scope belongs in the same sentence as the verdict, and it is narrower than §3's.**
The gate's corpus is text plus an unfiltered `{{ a }}` and nothing else — no tag appears
anywhere in it — while §3's corpus is `{% if %}` / `{% for %}` throughout. The two do not
overlap, and §3's declines at its first token. So the gate bounds the design **from below
on a subset**, and the region §3 measured its 7× parse win in is unmeasured at render.

Two probes run after the gate, both stock-side or floor-side against `.django/` @ 6.0.9,
both in `.review/m18-measurements/`, and both of which narrow the verdict further:

**The margin inverts with lookup depth.** Five rows of 154 variables each, identical
output bytes, differing only in how deep the lookup goes:

| lookup | floor/var | stock/var | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| compile-time literal | 1.114 | 0.373 | 2.966 |
| `{{ a }}` — what the gate measured | 1.111 | 1.169 | 0.949 |
| `{{ a.b }}` | 1.269 | 1.256 | 1.007 |
| `{{ a.b.c }}` | 1.376 | 1.293 | 1.062 |
| `{{ a.b.c.d }}` | 1.518 | 1.371 | 1.109 |

`{{ a }}` is the floor's *best* case, not its worst, and §3's `LARGE` uses
`section{i}.field{j}` — depth 1 — where the two arms are level. The mechanism is that
every lookup component is itself a crossing: the marginal step costs the floor 0.135 µs
against stock's 0.067, because stock is already inside an interpreter frame and the floor
is not. The floor wins at depth 0 only because stock's `Context.__getitem__` costs more
than one crossing; from the second component on it is paying a round trip for what stock
pays a bytecode for.

**A loop's cost is invisible to a node census.** One body copy over `n` iterations renders
the same bytes as `n` unrolled copies, at every `n` from 1 to 200, and stock's loop control
measures **≈ 2.0 µs fixed + 0.242 µs per iteration** (`transcript-forloop-axis.txt`). A
`{% for %}` is **one node** at every `n`. This is the axis the census cannot see, and it is
where §3's motivating number lives.

**The crossover is at 0.85 steps, not at 1.** The floor's whole depth-0 advantage is
0.058 µs per variable and an extra lookup component costs it 0.068 more than stock —
0.136 against 0.067 — so **less than one component consumes the entire margin**. The
render win exists for variables that are bare names and is already slightly negative
against a one-dot variable. §3's corpus has no bare names at all: `bench.py` writes
`section{i}.field{j}`, `section{i}.enabled`, `section{i}.label`, `item.name`,
`item.value`, every one at depth 1, and the loop body's two are depth 1 *per iteration*.

**That 0.85 is the least durable number in this section, and it is the one that gets
quoted.** The table above is a single run — the run it was taken from was not saved —
and five re-runs of the same probe put `{{ a }}` between **0.945 and 0.965** and
`{{ a.b }}` between **1.007 and 1.020**. The sign and the monotonicity hold in every
one of them; what moves is the crossover, because it is `(1 − r₀) / (r₁ − r₀)` and its
denominator is a difference of about 0.06 that the numerator divides into, so a
thousandth either side moves it by ~0.02 steps. Re-derived from the re-runs it is
**0.73**, not 0.85. The claim to carry out of this section is that a one-dot variable
is already the crossover and that §3's corpus is entirely one-dot variables — not the
second decimal. `transcript-lookup-depth.txt` in `.review/m18-measurements/` is one of
the re-runs, so the spread is re-derivable rather than asserted here.

**What that leaves open is the next slice, and it is a fork whose winning arm has to be
weighted.** Stock renders a loop at 1.436 µs per item against 1.194 unwrapped, and the
floor's per-item cost at depth 0 is 1.111. If the loop variable lives in a Rust-side
scope the floor is ahead by about 23%; if it is pushed onto the Python `Context` it pays
roughly three crossings per iteration and falls behind by about the same. But the overlay
arm is **not simply available**: an overlay living only in Rust is invisible to any node
delegated to Python, and one delegated node in a body forces the variable into the
`Context` for that node — at which point the push is paid anyway. So the realistic outcome
is mixed per loop, and the arm's applicability is the fraction of *loop bodies* that are
entirely Rust-handled. Census per body, parse-only, over §3's own corpus:

| template | loop bodies | overlay applies |
|---|---|---|
| `BASE` | 0 | — |
| `PAGE` | 1 | **0/1** — the body holds a `BlockTranslateNode`, and its per-iteration variable is filtered (`row.value\|floatformat:2`) |
| `LARGE` | 20 | **20/20** — every body is `TextNode×3 + VariableNode×2` |

So the weight is a property of the template, not of the design, and it is 20/20 on the
template §3's parse number was taken on and 0/1 on another (`transcript-body-census.txt`).
`PAGE` is also the design's
worst case and worth keeping as a row rather than a footnote: a body that is both
unhandled *and* filtered per iteration (`row.value|floatformat:2`) crosses the boundary a
third way, distinct from both the lookup chain and the escaper call.

**The overlay does not delete Django's loop control, it replaces it** — a Rust loop still
has to obtain item *N* from a Python sequence, bind it and step — so the 0.242 µs is an
upper bound and not the saving. With that term included:

| Rust's own per-iteration cost | net per iteration | share of the naive 0.216 |
|---|---|---|
| 0.000 (GIL held across the loop, direct index) | +0.216 | 100% |
| 0.067 (one bytecode-equivalent) | +0.149 | 69% |
| 0.135 (one `attach`/`unbind` `get_item` crossing) | +0.081 | 38% |

**The sign holds at every value in that range**, which is the robust half and the part to
state: on `LARGE`'s bodies the overlay is positive whatever Rust's iteration costs,
between +0.081 and +0.216 µs per iteration. It is the magnitude that is open. And the
value is a *choice* rather than a property of the tree — `PyTarget::get_item` pays
`Python::attach` + `get_item` + `unbind` per call, which is right for one-off access and
would be paid *per iteration* by a loop that reuses it, while a loop holding the GIL
across all iterations indexes near the bottom of the range. A for-loop slice that reuses
the existing helper therefore lands at the expensive end **by default**.

That is the point at which to stop costing and start measuring. Three terms here are
composed rather than measured — the Python-*method* call cost, the overlay's applicability
to bodies Rust has not yet been taught, and Rust's own iteration cost — and all three are
settled by implementation choices rather than by measuring this tree, so refining them
buys nothing until a design is picked. The one worth buying first is the Python *method*
call (`Context.push`/`pop`), because it is the term that cannot be chosen: it bounds the
fallback arm for exactly the bodies `PAGE` shows are a real population.

**M18.1 — self-hosting `_impl`.** Relative imports, translated name strings, an
importable identity, the backend class, the loader wiring.

**M18.2 — the boundary.** Third-party tag and filter handles, the context
crossings, `{% load %}`.

**M18.3 — the differential corpus**, at a size that makes the claim "renders
identically" mean something.

**M18.4 — packaging.** The wheel, the README rewritten around `TEMPLATES.BACKEND`,
the deletion of §5, and `scripts/check_packaging.sh` rewritten around a real
project that names the backend in settings and renders.

## 8. Non-goals

- **Not dropping `django.template` support.** The finder and `install()` survive as
  an opt-in developer mode, and §6 item 2 is why: without them the 1547 cannot run
  at all, and it is the only oracle here that was written by people who had never
  heard of this project. The cost is the maintenance of a second engine, which §9
  item 1 has to price.
- **Not a new template language.** Jinja and minijinja are structure references
  (compiled tree, renderer that calls back for host values), not behaviour
  references. Every construct renders as Django renders it, byte for byte.
- **Not abi3, not PyPI, not CI.** Out of scope as before.
- **Not tuning the shim.** The M16 levers are dead ends and §7 of that record says
  so.

## 9. Open questions

Review has now settled five: §5's deletions are deferred until after M18.0 and after
§6's comparison, because the fallback is the shim itself; the `host.py` identity
machinery is explicitly conditional on the shim surviving, which the first draft had
contradicting §6; §6 argues from authorship rather than coverage; the gate is on the
**dispatch-free floor** rather than on a threshold for the renderer, because the
first version's rebuttal was always in hand and so it could not stop anything; and
§3.1's node-class census now carries its method, which changes the answer from one bare
"43" to three counts — 53 emission sites, 44 distinct literals, 40 `NODE_CLASSES`
entries. That last revision is worth recording as a caution: the first correction to
this census was *itself* wrong, because the reviewer and I had each measured a different
quantity that happened to equal 43 — call sites of one constructor form, versus distinct
names across both. A count equal to a nearby count's size is not corroboration; it is
the case in which a breakdown is mandatory, and here it took a third number (53, which
neither of us had) to make the two 43s distinguishable. Three of the original questions
were not judgement calls and were measured instead: inheritance (§4.1), render-time
template resolution (§4.2), and the census itself.

What is still open:

1. **Does anything compare `_impl` to the Rust tree?** §6 keeps `_impl` alive as the
   transcription oracle, but the differential corpus renders through the *backend*,
   so on the current plan nothing compares the two engines to each other. Either
   something does, or `_impl` exists only to answer "is the transcription still
   Django's" and the Rust tree is trusted on the differential corpus alone. That is
   a decision to make, not a residue to inherit — and it is the maintenance cost §8
   now names.
2. **Where does route (b) go?** §3.1 offers rendering off the descriptors (a) or
   making the tree semantic (b). If (a) passes the gate, (b) is still worth doing —
   it deletes the descriptor and the materialiser outright — at the price of 39
   producers, the two helper-emitting sites, and the parser tests that assert
   `class_name`. Is that M18.1, or does it wait until the backend works end to end?
3. **`{% ssi %}`-shaped tags.** §4.2 enumerates the class of constructs that resolve
   a template during a render and probes two members. A user tag that resolves a
   template by name is a third, and the two measured members disagree about how to
   recognise a template (duck-typed vs `isinstance`). The class should be enumerated
   and probed together before the boundary work in M18.2 is called done.
